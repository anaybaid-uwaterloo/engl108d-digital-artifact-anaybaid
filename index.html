The multiple versions of you living online

01

Who are you, actually?

You probably have at least three completely different versions of yourself online right now. LinkedIn you who apparently "thrives in collaborative environments." Instagram you with the grid you've spent way too much time thinking about. And that anonymous Reddit or Discord account where you actually say what you're thinking.

We've always acted different around different people. You don't talk to your boss or superiors the way you talk to your friends. But social media changed something big. Now these performances stick around. They're searchable. Different audiences who have no business seeing the same version of you can all watch at once.

Smith and Watson call our profiles "virtual prosthetics." Your online presence works like a digital body part that helps you exist in these spaces, but none of this is neutral. Every choice (your avatar, your bio, which photos you post versus the 47 you immediately delete) is you deliberately building an identity.

Calculated Authenticity

The Algorithmics nailed this in the class glossary, with the mention of "How one presents oneself in a specific manner in order to appear 'genuine'. It may involve manufacturing one's image based on how they want to be seen, not how they really are."

You're not actually being fake, it's that you're being strategic about which parts of yourself you show. That casual caption? Took ~15 minutes. That natural photo? Attempt 27 and counting further. We're all performing authenticity which is honestly kind of absurd.

DataReportal found 5.24 billion people on social media as of February 2025. That's practically over 2 hours daily per person. That's not just mindless scrolling (okay some of it is). But mostly that's billions of people spending hours every day building these digital versions of themselves.

03

What's actually happening here

Networked Publics

Context collapse is essentially the biggest problem. Everyone basically sees everything - be it your work colleagues from Co-op terms, friends from high school, your parents (unless they are blocked), that person you met once on a Friday at a King Street frat party. They're all in the same audience. So you perform a version that kind of works for everyone, which means it's probably not authentic to anyone.

Platform architecture matters more than we'd ever want to admit. Van Dijck makes the precise argument in his central thesis that platforms aren't neutral tools. They're designed with specific rules and features that shape what you can even do. TikTok's algorithm rewards, for example, certain content and identities. LinkedIn forces you into rigid categories which we all know as Education, Experience, Skills. The platform itself is co-writing who you get to be on it.

The Datafied Self

There's another version of you that you didn't consciously create. Platforms build it from your data primarily from browsing history and your activity. What posts you pause on versus scroll past, how long you watch videos, etc. On their end, ethically or not, they're building shadow profiles from your behavior.

This datafied version serves platform profits, not you. It's for ad targeting, content curation, engagement optimization. You don't control it & can't even see it. But it's shaping what content you're shown and what ads follow you around.

McLuhan's "the medium is the message" really fits here. Social media as a medium teaches us that identity is performative. Always flexible, always under construction. Every platform becomes a stage and every post a choice about which version of yourself you're showing.

04

The authenticity problem

We work really hard to look effortless. Carefully planning casual posts, writing and rewriting captions to sound spontaneous. Literally performing authenticity to get this image.

You're not lying. You're just choosing which truths to show.

Questions worth asking yourself:

  • How many tries did your last profile picture actually take?
  • What percentage of photos you take actually get posted?
  • Do you act different across platforms?
  • What parts of your life never make it online?
  • Ever skip doing something because it wouldn't photograph well?
  • Who can't afford to maintain these curated identities?

Georgakopoulou argues posting selfies and self content is legitimate identity work. We tell stories about ourselves through carefully selected moments. The problem isn't curation, it's that we don't admit we're doing it and that too not even to ourselves.

But there's a bigger issue, which is that not everyone gets to play this game equally. Can't afford a good phone camera? Your Instagram aesthetic suffers. Have a disability that makes typing difficult? Maintaining text-heavy profiles across platforms becomes exhausting. From outside Western countries? The "authenticity" standards are often based on American norms that don't fit your context, and are subjected to unecessary racial "norms" as seen so commonly, unfortunately.

05

Who actually benefits from this?

Multiple digital identities aren't actually dishonest. We code switch in real life constantly, different contexts need different presentations. You don't quite act identical with your superiors versus let's say your best friend.

But here's the uncomfortable question: who actually benefits from all this performance? Every minute you spend curating your Instagram grid is practically engagement data for a muti-billion dollar corporation. Every revision of a LinkedIn headline is behavioral information. Every carefully chosen Discord username is a data point. We're doing unpaid labor that platforms monetize for their advantage(s).

When Instagram's interface dictates how you can present yourself, when TikTok's algorithm decides which version of you gets amplified, how much control do you actually have? Platform designs aren't neutral. They're built to maximize engagement, which maximizes their ad revenue. Your identity fragmentation in that jungle serves their profit model.

That datafied version of you exists whether you build it or not, whether you choose it or not (unfortunately). Platforms know things about you that you've never stated. They infer interests from what you pause to look at, construct shadow identities from your footprints. And Noble's research shows these algorithmic systems have bias built in. Not all identity performances are equally visible as some get suppressed, made invisible, or actively harmed by the same systems others benefit from.

This connects back to Week 4 discussions about identity online. Avatars and usernames work as identity claims. You spend 20 minutes picking a Discord username? You're making a statement about how you want to be recognized in that space. But you're also participating in a system that extracts value from your participation while giving you only the illusion of control.

What this actually means

This project started simple, with the idea that you have multiple versions of yourself online. But after examining Smith and Watson's virtual prosthetics, The Algorithmics' calculated authenticity concept from the Glossary section, Pérez-Torres' digital social mirror framework, and data about billions spending hours daily on this, it's clear digital identity construction isn't shallow by any means. It's maybe one of the most significant things we do in this day and age.

We're all performing, that's unavoidable. The question isn't whether to perform, it's whether you're aware you're doing it. Whether you're being intentional with it and whether you're thinking critically about who benefits from platforms controlling what's even possible.

Every time you edit a caption, pick a profile photo, decide whether to post something, you're doing some form of identity work. Making choices about which version of yourself to project. That's not shallow, but it's also not neutral. These choices happen within systems designed to extract value from your participation. Systems with biases about whose identities matter.

The real work isn't perfecting your performances. It's understanding the stages you're performing on and who built them. It's asking who gets excluded entirely, recognizing that constant identity curation serves platform profits more than it serves you. That's the uncomfortable truth underneath all those carefully curated grids.
