The multiple versions of you living online

01

Who are you, actually?

Think about it. You probably have at least three completely different versions of yourself online right now. There's LinkedIn you, who apparently "thrives in collaborative environments." Instagram you with the carefully planned grid. And then there's that anonymous Reddit or Discord account where you actually say what you're thinking.

This isn't totally new behavior, right? We've always acted different around different people. You don't talk to your boss the way you talk to your friends. But social media changed something fundamental. Now these performances are permanent. Searchable. Visible to audiences who really shouldn't all be watching the same version of you.

Smith and Watson talk about "virtual prosthetics" in their reading. Basically your online profiles work like digital body parts that help you exist in online spaces. But here's the thing: these extensions aren't neutral. Every choice you make (your avatar, your bio, which photos you post versus which ones you immediately delete) is deliberate identity construction.

Calculated Authenticity

The Algorithmics defined this in the class glossary and it's honestly the perfect term: "How one presents oneself in a specific manner in order to appear 'genuine'. It may involve manufacturing one's image based on how they want to be seen, not how they really are."

You're not being fake exactly. You're being strategic about which parts of yourself you show. That caption that sounds casual? Probably took 15 minutes to write. That natural looking photo? Maybe attempt 27. We're all performing authenticity which is kind of absurd when you stop and think about it.

DataReportal found 5.24 billion people on social media in 2024. Each person spending over 2 hours daily on these platforms. That's not just mindless scrolling (okay some of it definitely is). But mostly? That's billions of people spending hours every single day building and maintaining digital versions of themselves.

02

Evidence: My actual profiles

Platforms literally force you to be different versions of yourself. Norman calls this "affordances" - platforms designed in ways that make some behaviors easy and others basically impossible. LinkedIn's interface makes you think in job titles and corporate buzzwords. Instagram's grid makes you obsess over how posts fit your aesthetic. Reddit's anonymity lets you say things you'd never attach your real name to.

I'm constructing different versions based on what each platform rewards and what each audience expects.

03

What's actually happening here

Let's talk about what goes through your head when you delete and repost an Instagram story for the third time because something about the first version felt wrong.

Context Collapse
Impression Management
Platform Affordances
Algorithmic Curation
Networked Publics

Context collapse is the biggest problem here. Everyone sees everything. Your boss. Friends from high school. Your parents. That person you met once at a party. They're all in the same audience now. So you perform a version that sort of works for everyone, which probably means it's not authentic to anyone.

Platform architecture matters more than we admit. Van Dijck argues platforms aren't neutral tools. They're designed with specific rules and features that shape what you can even do. TikTok's algorithm rewards certain content types and identities. LinkedIn literally forces you into rigid categories: education, experience, skills. The platform itself is co-writing who you get to be on it.

The Datafied Self

There's another version of you that you didn't consciously create. The identity platforms build from your data. Browsing history. What posts you pause on versus scroll past. How long you watch videos. Platforms build shadow profiles from your behavior, creating an identity you never explicitly chose to project.

This datafied version serves platform profits, not you. It's used for ad targeting, content curation, engagement optimization. You don't control it or even see it. But it's shaping what content you're shown and what ads follow you around the internet.

McLuhan's famous "the medium is the message" really applies here. Social media as a medium teaches us that identity is performative. Flexible. Always under construction. Every platform becomes a stage. Every post a choice about which version of yourself you're projecting.

04

The authenticity problem

Here's what's weird. We work really hard to look effortless. Carefully planning casual posts. Writing and rewriting captions to sound spontaneous. Literally performing authenticity.

You're not lying. You're just strategically choosing which truths to show.

Questions worth asking yourself:

  • How many tries did your last profile picture take?
  • What percentage of photos you take actually get posted?
  • Do you act different across platforms?
  • What parts of your life never make it online?
  • Ever skip doing something because it wouldn't photograph well?
  • Who can't afford to maintain these curated identities?

Georgakopoulou argues posting selfies and self content is legitimate identity work. We tell stories about ourselves through carefully selected moments. The problem isn't curation itself. It's that we don't admit we're doing it - not even to ourselves.

But there's a bigger issue here. Not everyone gets to play this game equally. If you can't afford a good phone camera, your Instagram aesthetic suffers. If you have a disability that makes typing difficult, maintaining text-heavy profiles across multiple platforms becomes exhausting labor. If you're from outside Western countries, the "authenticity" standards are often based on American cultural norms that don't fit your context.

05

Who actually benefits from this?

Multiple digital identities aren't dishonest. We code switch in real life all the time. Different contexts need different presentations. You don't act identical with your boss versus your best friend.

But here's the uncomfortable question: who actually benefits from all this performance? Every minute you spend curating your Instagram grid is engagement data. Every revision of a LinkedIn headline is behavioral information. Every carefully chosen Discord username is a data point. We're doing unpaid identity labor that platforms monetize.

When Instagram's interface structures how you can present yourself, when TikTok's algorithm decides which version of you gets amplified, how much agency do you actually have? Platform designs aren't neutral. They're built to maximize engagement, which maximizes ad revenue. Your identity fragmentation across platforms serves their profit model.

That datafied version of you exists whether you build it or not. Platforms know things about you that you've never stated. They infer interests from what you pause to look at. They construct shadow identities from your digital footprints. And Noble's research shows these algorithmic systems have bias built in. Not all identity performances are equally visible or valued. Some get suppressed, made invisible, or actively harmed by the same systems others benefit from.

This connects back to Week 4 when we discussed identity online. Avatars and usernames function as identity claims. When you spend 20 minutes picking a Discord username, you're making a statement about how you want to be recognized in that space. But you're also participating in a system that extracts value from your participation while giving you only the illusion of control.

What this actually means

This project started with a simple observation: you have multiple versions of yourself online. But after examining Smith and Watson's virtual prosthetics, The Algorithmics' calculated authenticity concept, Pérez-Torres' digital social mirror framework, and data about billions spending hours daily on this, it's clear digital identity construction isn't shallow. It's maybe one of the most significant things we do in 2024.

We're all performing. That's unavoidable. The question isn't whether to perform. It's whether you're aware you're doing it. Whether you're being intentional with your choices. And whether you're thinking critically about who benefits from the platforms structuring what's even possible.

Every time you edit a caption, pick a profile photo, decide whether to post something, you're doing identity work. Making choices about which version of yourself to project into digital space. That's not shallow. But it's also not neutral. These choices happen within systems designed to extract value from your participation. Systems with built-in biases about whose identities matter.

The real work isn't perfecting your performances. It's understanding the stages you're performing on and who built them. It's asking who gets excluded from these performances entirely. It's recognizing that constant identity curation serves platform profits more than it serves you. That's the uncomfortable truth underneath all those carefully curated grids.
